\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}
\begin{spacing}{1.2}
\noindent
Thirty years after the dissolution of the Soviet Union, fertility across its successor
states has not converged. This thesis assembles a balanced panel of fourteen post-Soviet
countries over 2000--2023 and documents a difference between Central Asia and the
remaining ten states that is present in every year of the period, with no crossing at any
point, and that ends the window wider than it began. Beneath a pooled dispersion series
that appears flat, the four Central Asian countries grew significantly more alike while
dispersion also rose among the other ten, although the second pattern is sensitive to the
removal of individual countries and to the exclusion of the most recent years; there is no
evidence of catch-up. After 2016 the
difference widens from 1.19 to 1.65 children per woman, and roughly seventy per cent of
that widening comes from decline outside Central Asia rather than increase within it.
Conditioning on lagged income, urbanisation, remittances and child mortality reduces the
estimated difference from 1.31 to 0.96 children per woman, so approximately a quarter of
it co-moves with observable macroeconomic conditions. No control is individually
distinguishable from zero, and within-country variation yields no stable association.
Because Central Asian regional identity and Muslim population share correlate at 0.83
across fourteen countries, the two cannot be separated, and the second half of the research
question is answered as a bounded elimination rather than an identification. All results
are correlational.
\end{spacing}
